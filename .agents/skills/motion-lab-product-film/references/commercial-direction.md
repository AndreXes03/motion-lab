# Commercial direction

Use while planning or improving a commercial. These are craft lessons from repeated LOOP feedback, translated into reusable decisions. They are not a claim that every audience wants the same aesthetic.

## 1. Design the opening around attention and meaning

Avoid a static wide view of a complete interface while the audience waits for something to happen. If the story starts with a brief, show only the relevant input and send action on a quiet field. Start close on the text forming; reveal the larger composition shortly before the click. Let assets enter with a short, understandable cursor or attachment gesture rather than a pile of unrelated windows.

For this kind of pullback, a normalized Gaussian velocity profile can give soft tails and a concentrated middle. Tune start, duration, center/sigma and pan together; a smooth curve cannot fix an oversized or mistimed move. Keep the meaningful text readable while the frame changes. Do not apply this move to every kind of opening.

Use the brand's accent in the opening glow rather than a stock blue/purple. A slow shift in that field can keep the frame alive without competing with the typing. Distinguish a designed background glow from optical bloom on the product.

## 2. Give each shot a different reason to exist

Judge the sequence as a contact sheet before polishing. Compare subject scale, axis, subject count/depth, silhouette, motion path, lighting and what is being revealed. Adjacent feature shots should usually differ on more than one of these dimensions. A deliberate match cut can preserve some of them for continuity.

| Shot family | What makes it useful | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface macro | A grazing pass across real texture, perforation, weave or a seam. Let the surface fill much of the image. | A distant whole-product view labelled “detail”; oversized plastic-looking grille geometry. |
| Mechanical macro | Move around a crown, hinge, ring or control while that part performs its function. | Repeating the hero orbit with a slightly longer focal length. |
| Side/depth array | Multiple products in a lateral row or depth stack; a near object crosses foreground and reveals the next. | Identical front-facing copies on a flat grid; camera crossing a housing. |
| Internal assembly | Documented layers separate on plausible axes and reveal meaningful internal construction. | Empty shells, interpenetration, floating decorative screws, invented real-product engineering. |
| Black/rim detail | Dark body with a few narrow moving reflections defining an edge, control or profile. | A uniformly lit grey object or a nearly blank frame whose shape cannot be understood. |
| Close wide perspective | A near foreground feature, substantial depth behind it, and real camera travel. | Changing FOV on a remote centered product and expecting an obvious perspective effect. |
| Graphic/product bridge | A product contour, movement, material change or mechanism drives the graphic transition. | An unrelated procedural screensaver between product shots. |
| Readable hero | A moment that makes the complete product and identity clear. | Making every shot a hero rotation, or using so many crops that the product is never understood. |

Not every film needs every family. Select those that demonstrate the product and vary their composition. For SaaS, the equivalent variety is input detail, interaction, changed result, context and clear product identity—not fake 3D hardware.

## 3. Shape energy instead of making everything fast

Plan an anticipation, a fast transfer and a legible recovery. Put a velocity peak near the cut when the transition needs impact; match outgoing/incoming screen direction and apparent scale. Carry momentum into a title, graphic or logo rather than treating each layer as a separate animation.

Continuous movement is a hierarchy. Give a shot one dominant movement, one supporting change and a quiet place to read. A hero can breathe through a moving light, changing reflection or slight parallax while the product settles. Avoid constant spinning, synchronized sine-wave motion on every channel and identical ease-in-out on every shot.

Use sharp cuts, motion continuity, occlusion, matching shapes or a purposeful light/graphic transition. Do not replace every edit with a whoosh, flash or dissolve. A hard cut at peak speed can feel more continuous than a long generic transition.

For a speed ramp, design the path and its velocity together. More blur is not more movement. Keep the intermediate travel physically clear and reserve readable frames for the product detail or message.

## 4. Make perspective and lighting visible

Perspective comes from camera proximity and spatial depth as well as lens angle. Pair FOV with real camera distance and subject-size compensation; avoid changing just one number. Check near-plane clipping, distortion of recognizable product features and camera clearance throughout the move. A preview-only fisheye shader is not proof of matching Cycles optics.

Art-direct a rig for each shot: broad soft reflection for one material, thin rim for another, grazing texture light, then a brighter graphic reveal. Reposition light sources or reflection cards instead of recoloring the same rig. Neutral shadow fill helps metal, rubber and cloth remain distinct under colored rims. Small animated changes in camera, object, lights and reflections can work together without all having equal strength.

For an abstract studio world, separate the 3D beauty from a clean 2D gradient when the chosen compositor supports it. Animate the gradient's center, angle or intensity subtly in the same direction as the implied camera/light movement. Keep contact shadows or occlusion only when the spatial situation needs them. Do not use an exposed finite ground slab as a default background.

Defocused environments are useful when context matters but background detail does not. Blur cannot hide a poor silhouette or a mismatched product. Check both a clean diagnostic render and the finished image.

## 5. Model and finish for the camera

Design enough construction to reward close framing: chassis, shell thickness, seals, seam hierarchy, recessed connectors, control ridges, chamfers, labels and attachment logic. Spend geometry on visible silhouette and real openings. Avoid adding detail that cannot survive the final frame size.

Differentiate functional surfaces through roughness, reflection shape, microtexture, edge response and color, not just separate base colors. Use satin molded housing, machined/brushed metal, rubber seals, thin woven fabric and clear components where appropriate. Match material scale to object scale.

Choose a deliberate contrast of finishes. A smooth, highly polished colored lacquer can be the expressive hero surface, supported by an absorbent textile and brushed or cut metal controls. Matte is not automatically more premium. For lacquer, remove conflicting orange-peel/bump maps, smooth the shoulder and lip tangents, and shape a few long reflections with area lights. Do not add microtexture everywhere simply to claim detail.

A speaker fabric should read as fine weave and subtle relief, not chunky rigid bars. A crown should have a believable grip and layered manufacturing detail. For a precise consumer-hardware direction, contrast a smooth near-black polished cap with a fine matte machined grip, a narrow chamfer and one functional recessed index mark. Shape highlights with softboxes; copying logos or adding chrome everywhere does not create precision. An engraved mark should change the surface or recess, not float above it like a sticker. An exploded shot needs visible internal purpose—basket, diaphragm, coil, magnet or circuit when supported by references—not merely extra floating discs.

If a textile still looks flat in the intended close-up, investigate construction before increasing contrast: crossing yarns, small openings, a dark acoustic backing, subtle strand variation and tension/curvature can matter more than a stronger normal map. True woven geometry is appropriate only when its scale survives the shot and the cost is justified. Check grazing light and a neutral inspection view for intersections, moire and plastic-looking fibres. A wider camera can be the better composition, but must not be the only repair for a defective surface.

Use a shallower depth of field selectively to isolate a crown, coating edge or component in depth. Focus should track that feature as the camera moves. Compare a deep and a shallow proof; keep the important mechanism, type and enough identity readable. Do not lower the f-stop on every shot, and do not use defocus to conceal unresolved geometry.

An authorized fictional product allows designed internal detail; a real product requires evidence or an explicit illustrative treatment. Do not redesign a customer's product while “improving” its finish.

## 6. Make procedural graphics belong to the product

Choose an anchor: projected contour, historical path, pivot, mechanism, live data or an actual state change. The graphic should be traceable to that anchor.

For a product trail, a useful move starts large near the camera, accelerates away, curves and rotates. Emit contours from its historical positions, retaining scale and orientation. Let bold major bands and finer secondary lines establish hierarchy. A moving emitter can make concentric patterns richer than a static center.

Slow the graphic passage enough for the relationship to register. Increase complexity through interaction, layering, deformation and depth—not only by adding faster, thinner lines. If the product disappears and the graphic could belong to any brand, rethink the bridge.

A procedural finish change can spread as attached islands or a wet edge over the surface. Its progress must follow the Finish gesture and object space, not float in screen space or change an unrelated shot. Keep emission coherent between seeks. Describe an animated noise mask as a procedural coating, not a fluid simulation.

## 7. Make the UI demonstrate cause and effect

Show the smallest useful fragment of interface at a readable size. A cursor, part of a panel, one selected control and the consequence can be enough. Omit decorative close icons, unrelated chrome and fake finished software. For a real app use the supplied UI faithfully; simplify only with clear illustrative intent.

Place the panel in available space or beside the feature it affects; compose the product and panel together. Small and essential does not mean illegible. Avoid covering the key mechanism or pinning every panel to the same central position over the same product angle.

| Control being shown | Consequence that should be unmistakable |
| --- | --- |
| Lens | Perspective and foreground depth visibly change, using actual camera proximity as well as angle. |
| Light | Reflection shape, contrast or rim position changes on a surface that reveals it. |
| Finish | Color/material changes on the surface, with the swatch and transition synchronized. |
| Motion/curve | The supported object/camera motion changes; preserve the curve demonstration rather than substituting a material effect. |
| A SaaS action | The relevant content/state changes into a truthful useful result. |

The cursor anticipates, engages, moves and releases. Give a send/action button an observable hover, a small press response and a release/confirmation that causes the next event. Synchronize the cursor contact, button state and result; a cursor landing on an unchanged button feels unfinished. Panel arrival, knob/number movement and the product's response form one gesture; avoid rigid slide-ins followed by an isolated mechanical slider.

If using frosted glass, composite a genuinely blurred sample of the image behind the panel with restrained dark tint, a fine edge and a small highlight. A translucent grey rectangle alone is not background blur. Test readability over both dark and bright shots. Keep the treatment understated; “glass” does not require a large floating 3D object.

## 8. Use type as part of the edit

Write short, specific benefits or a meaningful bridge. Avoid filler such as “make it move more” if it adds no information. Maintain a consistent type system while varying composition, scale and brand/contrast color deliberately. A sequence of identical centered white titles flattens the edit.

White type can be a strong deliberate choice; repeated central placement is the problem to investigate, not white itself. Compose the type with the product's silhouette and negative space. Compare the same frame with a title beside the product, above a shoulder, or split across its two sides. Splitting a short phrase around the product can make it the visual bridge between the words; keep reading order and a visible gap from key edges. Review the layout throughout the object's motion, not only at its best pose.

Select the font using the actual words and case. “Tech” does not require a stencil, sci-fi or aggressively condensed face. A simple grotesque with distinctive terminals, counters or a few unusual lowercase letters can supply enough character. Show mixed-case and lowercase samples when these details carry the appeal; an all-caps comparison may hide the very difference the founder likes. Start with two or three materially different candidates, retain the user's favorite as a comparison anchor, and show more only when requested. Judge weight and spacing at the final viewing size. Verify the family from source or an authoritative specimen rather than guessing from a screenshot; keep the licensed source and do not embed a commercial webfont merely because another site serves it.

For choreography, read [Typography and motion](typography-and-motion.md): it distinguishes word travel, letter assembly, reading drift and exit, with alternatives to the automatic left-to-right reveal. Keep headline visibility separate from product/UI/brand so a clean version retains the workflow and closing identity.

## 9. Let music organize the pacing

Select or preserve the musical direction before locking the cut. For a high-energy launch, use a genuinely energetic track if available and authorized; generic corporate rhythm will not create the same feel. Calm or trustworthy briefs may need a different choice.

When a useful drop exists, audition approximately 3–4 seconds before it as the opening build, then land the visual reveal on the drop. Adjust to the actual hook length rather than adding empty seconds. Mark real downbeats, breaks, transients and phrase changes. A BPM grid is a guide, not evidence of perceptual synchronization.

Put major transitions and speed peaks on chosen accents; not every beat needs a cut. Leave breathing room for the product proof and UI. A quiet section can still move. Use a few purposeful, low-level sound effects; listen to SFX separately and in the mix.

Preserve an approved track and trim unless asked otherwise. If alternatives are requested, use existing usable tracks first; recut/retime deliberately and inspect each result. Optical-flow interpolation can deform product edges, UI, type and rings. Prefer existing frames or native rerendering for those passages, and inspect actual compressed output.

Do not infer rights or paid-generation permission from a music reference. A reference can communicate energy without being the deliverable soundtrack.

## 10. End with continuity and restraint

Choose a recognizable product feature or path to hand motion to the brand. Match position, screen direction and apparent scale at the cut; a slight fast initial logo scale settling toward its final size can complete the motion. Do not morph or redraw the official logo just to force a match.

A dark product retreat can resolve to a clean black brand frame when appropriate. Other palettes can resolve to their own quiet field. Remove unnecessary final supers. Give the logo and any chosen CTA a readable interval; do not turn the close into the slowest, most cluttered part of the film.

Use brand-only when that is the brief. For founder acquisition, use the one chosen next action if needed. Do not copy Framevolve's logo or LOOP's closing message into another customer's commercial.

## 11. Finish lightly and keep refinement possible

Use motion blur to connect fast travel while preserving detail in slower sections. Tune shutter length independently from sampling. Do not blend samples across hard cuts. More blur is not an excuse for repeated or uninteresting framing.

Treat chromatic aberration and glow as finishing, with an off switch. In LOOP, up to roughly 1 px of edge aberration and a compact roughly 2 px highlight glow at 1080p were useful restrained targets; they are not universal settings or automatic properties of every renderer. Scale for output resolution and inspect a crop. Avoid broad bloom fog and apply optics to the beauty before crisp UI/text where the pipeline supports that separation.

Expose meaningful controls for later refinement: shot-local timing, camera distance/lens, separate product and camera motion, light balance, roughness/color, title entrance/exit/stagger, zoom softness and procedural density. Put advanced curve handles behind a focused workspace. Unsupported sliders are not an editable workflow.

Keep a short feedback ledger by scene. Record the intended change, accepted details to preserve and evidence from the new preview. When feedback repeats—“same angles,” “too rigid,” “too slow,” “cheap materials”—repair that underlying pattern across affected shots instead of treating each mention as a cosmetic one-off.
